Practical Calculation Guides

Learn how to solve everyday financial, health, and household calculations with simple formulas and worked examples. These guides explain the basics so you can understand your results instead of relying on numbers alone.

1. How to Calculate a Percentage

A percentage expresses a number as a fraction of 100. It is commonly used to compare prices, scores, expenses, and changes in value.

Percentage = (Part ÷ Whole) × 100

Example: You answered 45 questions correctly out of 60. Your score is (45 ÷ 60) × 100 = 75%.

To find 15% of 200, multiply 200 by 0.15. The result is 30.

2. How to Calculate a Discount

A discount reduces the original price of a product or service. Knowing the discount amount helps you compare offers and plan your spending.

Discount = Original Price × Discount Rate

Sale Price = Original Price − Discount

Example: An item costs $100 and has a 20% discount. You save $20 and pay $80 before any additional taxes or fees.

Remember that a discount percentage is usually applied to the original price unless the offer states otherwise.

3. How to Calculate Body Mass Index (BMI)

Body Mass Index (BMI) is a screening measure that compares weight with height. It can provide a general indication of weight status in adults, but it does not directly measure body fat or overall health.

BMI = Weight (kg) ÷ Height (m)²

Example: At 70 kg and 1.75 m tall, BMI = 70 ÷ (1.75 × 1.75) ≈ 22.9.

For adults, a BMI from 18.5 to 24.9 is commonly classified as the healthy-weight range. Interpretation may differ for children, teenagers, pregnant people, and some athletes.

BMI is not a diagnosis. Speak with a qualified healthcare professional for an individual assessment.

4. How to Calculate Monthly Loan Payments

A fixed-payment loan is commonly repaid through regular installments covering principal and interest. Your payment depends on the amount borrowed, interest rate, and repayment period.

M = P × r × (1 + r)n ÷ ((1 + r)n − 1)

Where:

  • M = monthly payment
  • P = principal borrowed
  • r = monthly interest rate as a decimal
  • n = total number of monthly payments

For example, a $10,000 loan with a 6% nominal annual interest rate repaid over five years has a monthly payment of about $193.33, assuming a fixed rate and monthly payments.

Actual payments may differ because of fees, insurance, rounding, or lender-specific terms. Compare the total amount repaid, not just the monthly payment.

5. Understanding Compound Interest

Compound interest occurs when interest is added to your balance and future interest is calculated on the growing balance. Over time, this can make savings or investments grow faster than with simple interest.

A = P × (1 + r)t

Where:

  • A = future value
  • P = initial principal
  • r = annual interest rate as a decimal
  • t = number of years

Example: $1,000 invested at 5% annually for 10 years becomes approximately $1,628.89, assuming annual compounding and no additional deposits or withdrawals.

Actual returns may vary. Investment returns are not guaranteed, and fees, taxes, and inflation can affect the final value.

6. How to Calculate Profit Margin

Profit margin shows what percentage of revenue remains as profit after the costs included in your calculation. It helps businesses evaluate pricing and financial performance.

Profit = Revenue − Total Costs

Profit Margin = (Profit ÷ Revenue) × 100

Example: A product generates $500 in revenue and has $300 in costs. Profit is $200, and the profit margin is ($200 ÷ $500) × 100 = 40%.

Profit margin and markup are different. Margin compares profit with revenue, while markup compares profit with cost. Make sure you include the relevant costs for your business.

7. How to Calculate Electricity Costs

Electricity consumption is commonly measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh). You can estimate the energy used by an appliance from its power rating and operating time.

Energy (kWh) = Power (W) × Hours Used ÷ 1,000

Cost = Energy Used (kWh) × Price per kWh

Example: A 100-watt appliance running for eight hours uses 0.8 kWh. At $0.15 per kWh, the estimated energy cost is $0.12.

This estimate excludes fixed service charges, taxes, and other fees that may appear on your electricity bill. Actual usage may also differ from the rated power.

Tips for Getting Reliable Results

  • Check that your units are correct before entering values.
  • Use the same currency when comparing prices and costs.
  • Confirm whether interest rates are annual or monthly.
  • Round only after completing the calculation.
  • Review assumptions before using estimates for financial decisions.
